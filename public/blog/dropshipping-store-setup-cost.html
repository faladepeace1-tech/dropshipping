How Much Does a Dropshipping Store Cost?

By Akinyemi Ifeoluwa · October 12, 2026 · 8 min read

A dropshipping store can cost a little or a lot, but most beginners underestimate the same three things: the platform, the apps and the money needed to find customers. This is a realistic breakdown.

The short answer

At Nexatech, a done-for-you build starts at $149 for the Starter package, $299 for Pro and $599 for Elite. That covers the store build, product research, supplier setup and payment integration. It does not cover your domain, platform subscription, apps, ad spend or product samples.

If you build it yourself, you can spend less in cash but much more in time. If you hire cheap help, you can spend less upfront and more later fixing the store. The right budget depends on which resource is scarcer for you.

1. Store build or theme

A theme is the cheapest design option. Our themes cost $39 to $59 and give you a design foundation. You still need to configure products, suppliers, payments and copy.

A done-for-you build costs more because it includes the work around the design: research, copy, supplier wiring, payment setup, testing and handover. The price is not only for a template. It is for the decisions that make the template usable.

2. Domain and hosting

A domain usually costs a small annual fee. The exact price depends on the extension and registrar. Avoid buying many similar domains at the start. One clear brand domain is enough.

Hosting is included in platforms like Shopify, so you do not pay separately for a server. If you use WooCommerce, you pay for hosting and maintenance instead. Compare the total cost, not just the monthly sticker.

3. Platform subscription

Shopify charges a monthly subscription and may charge transaction fees depending on your payment setup. WooCommerce is free as software, but plugins, hosting and maintenance add up. Neither is free overall.

Do not choose a platform only because the monthly fee is lower. A platform that makes checkout harder or breaks on mobile will cost more in lost sales than you save.

4. Apps and tools

Most stores need a small stack: supplier integration, reviews, email or SMS automation, analytics and maybe a currency converter. App subscriptions can quietly become the largest recurring cost.

Start with the minimum. Add an app only when you can name the problem it solves and the number you expect it to improve. Cancel tools you do not use.

5. Products and samples

You do not buy inventory upfront in dropshipping, but you should budget for samples. A sample lets you check quality, packaging and delivery time. If the product is fragile or skin-contact, a sample is not optional.

You also need a small reserve for supplier shipping and any replacement orders. Refunds and reships are part of retail.

6. Payment processing

Payment providers charge a percentage and sometimes a fixed fee per transaction. Cross-border payments may add conversion costs. Build these into your price instead of discovering them after a sale.

Some providers also hold a reserve or delay payouts for new accounts. Check the payout schedule before you rely on the money for ads.

7. Advertising and content

This is the line most beginners underestimate. A store with no traffic makes no sales. You need either time to create organic content or money to test paid ads. Often you need both.

Set a testing budget you can afford to lose. Do not use rent money or borrowed money for ads. The purpose of the first budget is to learn, not to get rich in a week.

8. Support and operations

Budget for your time. Customer messages, supplier follow-ups, refunds and content take hours. If you outsource, that is a real cost. If you do it yourself, protect the time in your week.

How to avoid wasting money

  • Do not buy a premium theme before you know the niche.
  • Do not pay for ten apps in the first week.
  • Do not spend the whole budget on design and leave nothing for traffic.
  • Do not buy courses that promise guaranteed results.
  • Do not scale ads before the product page converts.

Is done-for-you cheaper?

Not always in cash, but often in time and mistakes. If you value your time and want the technical setup done correctly, a done-for-you build can be cheaper than months of trial and error. If you have more time than money, building it yourself is a valid path.

Compare the deliverables, not just the price. Ask what happens after handover, who owns the accounts and what support is included. A cheap build with no handover is not cheap.

Frequently asked questions

Can I start dropshipping with a very small budget?

You can start with less if you build it yourself and use organic traffic. But you still need a domain, a platform and enough time to learn. Do not expect paid ads to work on a budget that only covers a few clicks.

What is the biggest hidden cost?

Advertising and failed payments. Both are easy to underestimate because they scale with activity, not with the initial build.

Do I need to pay for Shopify and a build?

Yes, if you choose Shopify. The build fee is for the service; the platform subscription is separate. We explain both before you buy.

What changes the final price

Two stores can look similar and cost very different amounts. The gap usually comes from scope, not from the platform. Before comparing quotes, list exactly what you expect the builder to deliver.

  • Product research depth. A shortlist of ideas is cheaper than validated products with supplier checks and margin analysis.
  • Number of products and variants. More products mean more copy, images, variants and quality checks.
  • Integrations. Payments, WhatsApp, email, analytics, reviews and supplier apps all add setup time.
  • Copy and creative. Original product copy and ad creatives cost more than a template with placeholder text.
  • Revisions and support. A fixed number of revision rounds is normal; unlimited revisions are not.
  • Speed and urgency. Rush delivery usually carries a premium because it displaces other work.

Budget beyond the build

The build fee is only one line in your first-month budget. Plan for the domain, platform subscription, apps, payment processing, content, ads and a small reserve for refunds or supplier issues. If your budget ends at the build fee, you have funded a store but not a launch.

A practical split is to treat the build as a fixed cost, then set a separate test budget for traffic and a separate reserve for operations. Do not use ad spend money to pay for setup upgrades at the last minute.

How to compare quotes fairly

Ask every provider for the same line items: products, pages, copy, images, integrations, tracking, training, revisions, handover and support window. A low quote that excludes half of those items is not comparable to a complete quote.

Also ask who owns the domain, store account, ad account, pixel and content after handover. Ownership should be in writing. If the builder keeps control of your accounts, you are renting a business rather than buying one.

Red flags in cheap quotes

  • No contract, no scope and no written handover checklist.
  • Proof that cannot be verified live or traced to a real store.
  • Hidden fees for domain, apps, revisions or account transfer.
  • Pressure to pay the full amount before any work is shown.
  • Promises of guaranteed sales or a fixed income.

Questions to ask before you pay

What exactly is included? What is excluded? How many revision rounds? Who owns the accounts? What happens if a supplier fails? How will I be trained? What support do I get after handover? What is the refund policy? A provider who answers these clearly is easier to work with than one who only sends a price.

Currency, payment fees and the real landed cost

If you sell in one currency and pay suppliers in another, exchange-rate movement and conversion fees can quietly reduce your margin. Build a small buffer into your pricing and review it when rates move. Also account for payment processing fees, refunds and chargebacks before you decide that a product is profitable.

Ask your builder whether the quoted price covers gateway setup, test transactions and currency display. Those details are easy to overlook and expensive to fix after launch.

What to keep in writing

Keep the scope, payment schedule, revision limit, handover date, ownership terms and support window in one document. A clear written agreement protects both sides and gives you something concrete to review when the project changes.
