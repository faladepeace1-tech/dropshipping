How to Start Dropshipping in Nigeria

By Akinyemi Ifeoluwa · October 18, 2026 · 9 min read

Dropshipping in Nigeria is not hard because of the idea. It is hard because of the details: reliable suppliers, payment approval, delivery expectations and trust. This guide walks through the parts that actually decide whether a new store sells or stalls.

1. Understand the model before you pick a product

Dropshipping means you sell a product and a supplier ships it directly to your customer. You do not buy stock upfront. Your profit is the difference between your selling price and the supplier's cost, minus payment fees, delivery costs and advertising.

That sounds simple, but the model has two jobs that cannot be skipped: finding customers and keeping them happy after they pay. A store is not a vending machine. It needs a clear offer, a trustworthy page and a way to handle questions, delays and refunds.

Before you think about logos or themes, write down three things: who you want to sell to, what problem the product solves and why someone would buy from you instead of a market or a bigger store. If you cannot answer those questions in one sentence each, the niche is not ready.

2. Choose a niche that fits Nigerian buying habits

Nigeria is a mobile-first market. Most shoppers will meet your store on a phone, often on mobile data, and they will decide quickly whether the page feels real. That makes fast pages, clear prices and visible trust signals more important than fancy animation.

Strong niche signals to look for:

  • Repeat purchase potential: skincare, haircare, supplements, pet supplies and consumables bring customers back.
  • Clear visual proof: fashion, gadgets and home products are easy to show in photos and short videos.
  • Reasonable delivery size: light, compact products are cheaper and easier to ship than bulky items.
  • Local demand: products people already search for, instead of products that need a long education.

Avoid niches where the only advantage is a low price. Someone will always undercut you. Compete on presentation, support and a specific customer instead.

3. Research products with real demand

Product research is not scrolling a supplier page and picking what looks interesting. Start with problems and buying language. Search social platforms, marketplaces and search engines for what people complain about, ask for and compare. Look for repeated questions such as "does it work for oily skin" or "will it fit a small room". Those questions become your product page copy.

Check four numbers before you commit:

  • Supplier cost: can you sell at a healthy multiple after fees and delivery?
  • Shipping time: can you promise a window you can actually keep?
  • Failure risk: how often does the product arrive damaged, wrong or unusable?
  • Repeat potential: is there a reason to buy again or buy a related item?

Order a sample when the margin allows it. A sample tells you more about packaging, quality and delivery than any supplier description.

4. Find suppliers you can rely on

You have two broad options: local suppliers and international suppliers. Local suppliers can deliver faster and make returns easier, but product variety may be limited and pricing can be higher. International suppliers offer more variety and lower unit costs, but delivery windows are longer and returns are harder.

Whichever you choose, ask the supplier:

  • How long have you been shipping to Nigeria?
  • What is the real delivery window, not the best case?
  • Who handles a damaged or missing order?
  • Can you provide tracking that updates?
  • Can you hold stock for a product that starts selling?

Do not rely on one supplier for your best product. Keep a backup, and note the backup in your own operations sheet. Supplier stock changes are normal.

5. Set up payments that your customers trust

Payment is where many Nigerian stores lose sales. Apply for a gateway that supports your business type, complete your verification and test the checkout with a real small transaction before launch. Display the payment methods clearly on the product page and in the footer.

If card approval is slow, do not secretly switch customers to a method they do not expect. Explain the options, keep the checkout honest and make sure every failed payment has a support path. Never ask a customer to send card details through WhatsApp.

For international customers, show the currency they expect and make sure the final charge is clear before confirmation. If you use crypto, present it as an option, not a requirement.

6. Build the store around trust

A dropshipping store has no physical shop window, so the page has to do the work of one. The essentials are simple: a clear product title, real photos, a short benefits section, delivery information, a return policy, contact details and reviews that are actually yours.

Do not invent reviews, awards or sales numbers. If you do not have proof yet, say what you do have: a real contact channel, a clear policy, a sample-tested product and a supplier you can name on request. Honesty converts better than fake urgency.

Also make the mobile experience fast. Compress images, remove unnecessary scripts and test the checkout on a slow connection. A beautiful page that takes ten seconds to load is a closed shop.

7. Price for profit, not for applause

New store owners often price low to look competitive, then discover the margin cannot cover ads, fees and refunds. Work backwards: supplier cost plus shipping plus payment fee plus expected refund cost plus ad cost plus your target profit. That is your minimum selling price.

If the number feels too high for the market, the product is wrong, not the math. A cheaper product with no margin is not a business.

8. Launch with one traffic channel

You do not need to be everywhere on day one. Pick one channel you can execute consistently. Short-form video works for visual products. Search content works for products people research. Paid ads work when you have a clear budget and can track results.

Set a testing budget you can afford to lose, define what a good result looks like before you spend, and give the test enough time to produce useful data. Do not change everything every day.

9. Handle delivery and support properly

Delivery is part of the product. Tell customers what to expect before they pay, send tracking when it is available, and answer messages within a reasonable window. When something goes wrong, solve it quickly. A customer who gets a fast resolution often becomes a repeat buyer.

Keep a simple record of orders, supplier costs, refunds and ad spend. You cannot improve what you do not measure.

10. Give it a real test period

A store does not prove itself in three days. Give the offer a fair test: enough traffic to learn, enough time to fix obvious problems and enough discipline to change one variable at a time. If the product gets clicks but no sales, fix the page and offer. If the page converts but the product gets returns, fix the product or supplier.

Most beginners quit at the point where the data is finally becoming useful. Decide in advance how long you will test and what result would make you stop.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a registered business to start dropshipping in Nigeria?

Payment providers and suppliers may ask for business registration depending on the account type. Check the requirements of your chosen gateway before you apply so you are not surprised.

How much money do I need?

You need a build budget, a domain and platform cost, and a separate testing budget for traffic. Keep these separate. Spending the entire budget on the store leaves nothing to find customers.

Can I dropship from Nigeria to other countries?

Yes, but delivery time, customs and payment options change by destination. Start with one target country and build the operation around it before expanding.

Is dropshipping still worth it?

It is worth it when you treat it as a real retail business with real customer service. It is not worth it if you expect passive income without testing, support or learning.

A realistic 30-day launch plan

A launch plan keeps you from spending weeks on low-value tasks. Treat the first month as a sequence of checkpoints, not a race to publish a store.

Week 1: choose the offer and validate demand

Pick one product category and one clear customer. Check that people already search for the problem, that competitors are running ads, and that the product can survive shipping and returns. Speak to at least five potential customers before you buy a domain.

Week 2: build the store and prepare operations

Set up the store, product pages, policies, payment options and WhatsApp support. Order a sample if time allows. Write your delivery promise, return rules and supplier escalation process so you are not inventing them during your first busy day.

Week 3: prepare traffic and tracking

Install analytics, a pixel and conversion tracking. Create five to ten ad angles, three to five creatives and a simple landing page for each offer. Set a daily test budget you can afford to lose without borrowing.

Week 4: launch, measure and fix

Launch with a small budget, review the data daily and fix the biggest leak first. Most new stores do not need more traffic; they need clearer offers, faster pages and better answers to buyer objections.

Nigeria-specific mistakes to avoid

  • Trusting screenshots without context. Ask for a live walkthrough, order history or a verifiable store link.
  • Ignoring delivery reality. Be clear about timelines, tracking and what happens when a supplier delays.
  • Pricing without landed cost. Include product cost, shipping, payment fees, returns and ad spend before setting a price.
  • Running everything through one personal chat. Keep order records, payment references and customer consent in a system you can review.
  • Launching without policies. Shipping, returns, privacy and contact details reduce disputes and build trust.
  • Buying followers or fake reviews. They do not create sales and they damage the one asset that matters: credibility.
