How to Get Your First 100 Dropshipping Customers

By Akinyemi Ifeoluwa · November 4, 2026 · 11 min read

The first 100 customers are not usually won by one clever ad. They come from a clear offer, repeated exposure to the right audience and a support experience that turns a first order into a second one.

Start with the math, not the channel

If your product sells for $35 and your contribution before acquisition is $18, you cannot spend $40 to get each customer and expect the store to survive. Write down the maximum you can pay for a customer, including refunds and support, before you choose a channel.

Then decide what “100 customers” means for your business. It could be 100 first-time buyers, 100 completed orders or 100 customers who receive the product successfully. A cancelled order is not a customer, and a refunded order is not proof of product-market fit.

Make the first offer easy to understand

Before traffic, a visitor should know who the product is for, what problem it solves, what it costs, when it arrives and what happens if it is wrong. If any of those answers is missing, every channel becomes less efficient.

  • A specific headline instead of a vague brand slogan.
  • Real product photos and a short demo.
  • Price, shipping cost and delivery range visible before checkout.
  • Clear return and contact information.
  • One primary call to action.

Channel 1: Organic short-form video

Short video works when it shows the product doing something useful. Film the problem, the product in use and one specific detail a still image cannot show. Post consistently enough to learn which angle gets saves, shares and questions.

Do not make every video a discount ad. Educational clips, comparisons, common mistakes and “who this is for” videos build trust and give you reusable creative for paid tests.

Channel 2: Small paid tests

Use a limited budget to test one audience, one offer and a few creative angles. Judge the test on the full path: click-through, product page engagement, add-to-cart and checkout completion. A high click rate with no purchases usually means the page or the promise is wrong.

Do not scale a campaign because it produced one sale. Look for a repeatable pattern across enough spend to be meaningful, and account for refunds before calling it profitable.

Channel 3: Micro-influencers and creators

Find creators whose audience already has the problem. A small creator with relevant comments can outperform a large account with generic reach. Give a clear brief, require disclosure and agree on deliverables before payment.

Track with a unique link or code. Judge the partnership on sales and qualified traffic, not likes. A free product plus a small fee may be enough for a first test, but the terms should be written down.

Channel 4: Search and marketplace intent

Google Shopping, search ads and marketplace listings reach people who are already looking. They can be expensive, but the intent is often stronger than interruptive social ads. Start with exact product terms and a landing page that matches the query.

Organic search takes longer. Publish product pages and guides that answer specific questions, then link them to the product. Do not create dozens of thin pages that repeat the same text.

Channel 5: Email and referrals

Capture email with a useful reason, such as a buying guide or a back-in-stock alert. Send a short welcome sequence that explains the product, delivery and returns. Do not buy a list or add people who did not opt in.

Ask happy customers for a referral after delivery, not before. A simple “share this with someone who has the same problem” can work better than a complicated loyalty program at this stage.

Channel 6: Communities, with permission

Forums, local groups and niche communities can produce early feedback and sales, but self-promotion rules vary. Join as a person, answer questions honestly and ask moderators before posting a link. Spam destroys trust faster than it creates orders.

Measure the channel, not just the sale

  • Cost per click and cost per add-to-cart.
  • Cost per completed order.
  • Refund and chargeback rate by source.
  • Support tickets per 100 orders.
  • Repeat purchase rate after 30 and 60 days.
  • Contribution margin after shipping, fees and refunds.

A channel that brings cheap orders but constant refunds is not cheap. A channel that brings fewer, better customers may be the one worth scaling.

A 90-day plan

Days 1–30: foundation

Fix the offer, build the core pages, order a sample, set up tracking and publish 10 pieces of useful content. Run a small paid test only after checkout works on mobile.

Days 31–60: learn

Test two channels and several creative angles. Ask every buyer what almost stopped them. Fix the most common objection. Start a simple abandoned-checkout email.

Days 61–90: repeat

Double down on the channel with the best contribution margin. Add a referral request and a post-purchase review request. Cut campaigns that create refunds or support problems.

What not to do

  • Buy followers or reviews.
  • Promise delivery times you cannot meet.
  • Discount from day one until the product has no perceived value.
  • Run ads to a page that has not been tested on a phone.
  • Ignore customer messages after the sale.
  • Scale spend before you know your refund rate.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get 100 dropshipping customers?

There is no fixed timeline. Some stores take months, and some never reach it with a weak offer. Focus on improving conversion and repeat purchase while testing channels you can measure.

Should I use influencers for my first sales?

Micro-influencers can be useful when their audience matches your customer and they disclose the partnership. Start with a small, clear brief and measure clicks and sales, not just views.

Are giveaways a good way to get customers?

They can build an email list, but many entrants only want the free item. Use a giveaway only when the prize attracts the same person who would buy, and follow the rules for your market.
